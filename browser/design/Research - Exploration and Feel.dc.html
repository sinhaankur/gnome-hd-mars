How Mars HAWC can reward exploring and feel heavier

What reviewers said G-NOME got right and wrong in 1997, what four modern games show about curiosity and impact, and which real Martian terrain is worth sending a pilot into.

Takeaway 1

Reviewers loved the hijack and hated how it played: it rarely worked under fire, and a pilot on foot died fast. Make the hijack reliable before adding anything else.

Takeaway 2

Impact comes from layered feedback: trauma-based shake, a few frames of hit-stop, sound and particles together. One guide warns that this polish cannot fix controls that feel slow.

Takeaway 3

Exploration works when terrain hides things and knowledge is the reward. Real Mars already offers both: MOLA relief to hide behind, and lava-tube skylights on Arsia Mons.

01 · The 1997 lesson

G-NOME's best idea was its least reliable one

Contemporary reviews agree on the fantasy. GameSpot praised leaving your vehicle, jumping into enemy machines and turrets, and using a stolen IFF code to avoid detection (GameSpot). The box copy promised being chased on foot by a four-storey HAWC, then ejecting its pilot with GASHR (Collection Chamber). MobyGames' reviewer called the ability to bail out of a damaged HAWC and take an enemy's "very exciting when it happens" (MobyGames).

The same reviewer said hijacking hardly ever worked mid-combat and that an ejected pilot was usually stomped or shot. GameSpot found the controls sluggish: keeping the torso on a circling enemy was nearly impossible, and on foot you fired the way you ran, not the way you faced. Collection Chamber adds that enemies aimed better than the player did.

G-NOME also had on-foot objectives. Some missions required you to leave the HAWC and infiltrate a building or guard tower (MobyGames). Retro Replay recalls hacking terminals and slipping into bunkers on foot to set charges (Retro Replay). That gives the game a precedent for exploration that only a pilot can do.

"…hijacking other vehicles — hardly ever works in the midst of combat."

MobyGames review of G-NOME

Where Mars HAWC stands: GASHR fires on foot within 50 m, and the ejected pilot runs. Level 1 now forces the swap with a scripted rail hit, so every player goes through the loop once.

02 · Feel

Weight comes from feedback stacked on responsive controls

The common reference points are Jonasson and Purho's 2012 GDC talk "Juice it or lose it" and Jan Willem Nijman's 2013 "The Art of Screenshake". Nijman's talk is a list of about thirty tricks that made Nuclear Throne feel violent, from muzzle flash to hit-stop to camera kick (Egmatic, A+ Games).

For camera shake, the standard method comes from Squirrel Eiserloh's GDC math talk. Shake is driven by a "trauma" value, its strength is trauma squared, and it fades smoothly. The same guide recommends pairing a brief hit-stop with particles and sound, and offering a shake setting for players prone to motion sickness (Ryu Artistry). Hit-stop means pausing a few frames when a hit connects. If it runs too long, combat feels sluggish (A+ Games).

Egmatic's caution applies directly to G-NOME's history: polish cannot save input lag, it only makes a laggy game louder. Mech games add their own tension. Titanfall alternates pilot and Titan every few minutes. Embarking lowers your top speed, raises your health and size, and that contrast keeps matches fresh (Giant Bomb).

"Polish cannot save input lag; it only makes a laggy game louder."

Egmatic, game feel guide

Where Mars HAWC stands: shake is a single decaying scalar, slow-motion runs on big kills, and there is no shake setting yet.

03 · Exploration

Hide things behind terrain, then reward the player with knowledge

Triangle rule. Nintendo's CEDEC 2017 talk described building Breath of the Wild's terrain from triangle shapes. Each one forces a choice between going over and going around, and creates surprise about what lies behind it (Game Developer). GMTK adds that the eye goes to the peak, so points of interest belong there. The triangles also block what is behind them, which keeps the player from being swamped by options (GMTK). Smaller structures between towers pull players off course, which the team called "gravity" (Nintendo Life).

Knowledge as the reward. Outer Wilds set out to move players through its world without missions or explicit direction (GDC Vault). Its creative director says each mystery needs about three clues, and two clues for one mystery never sit on the same planet (interview). A ship log acts as a guaranteed minimum record, so clues found out of order still add up.

Terrain as an opponent. In Death Stranding, rivers and mountains are real obstacles, so route planning becomes play and every trip is a small risk-management problem (25YL). One critic argues that the late-game vehicles pave over the friction that made the walking good (Luke Shaw). Opinion is split, and another reviewer calls it walking between radio towers at a slower pace.

Where Mars HAWC stands: the heritage probes give a one-line message when you get near. There is no log, no scanner and nothing to piece together.

04 · Real Mars

Arsia Mons has holes only a pilot can go down

HiRISE imaged a pit about 150 m across on the north slope of Arsia Mons. It has no raised rim, its wall is nearly vertical, and its floor was still out of sight at least 78 m down (NASA APOD, 2007). ESA's caves team describes a skylight on the southern flank about 200 m wide and 80 m deep. It also describes chains of skylights and pit craters that can be traced for more than 50 km to the lava outflows (ESA).

The science is unsettled. Later HiRISE images of one Arsia pit showed a side wall, which suggests a dead-end shaft about 178 m deep rather than a cave entrance (Phys.org). Researchers still discuss caves like these as possible future shelter for astronauts. A mission built on them can stay honest by showing this uncertainty in the briefing.

05 · Proposed next batch (my recommendations, not findings)

Six pieces, in build order

  1. Hijack you can trust. Add aim assist for GASHR, a short window where nearby HAWCs lose track of a pilot who has just ejected, and cover near every hijack target. This answers the 1997 complaint first.
  2. Trauma shake and hit-stop. Replace the single shake value with trauma squared plus noise, aimed in the direction of the hit. Add 40 to 80 ms of hit-stop on armour breaks and kills, and a 0 to 100% shake setting in the new Settings panel.
  3. Field log and scanner. On foot, the pilot scans probes, rock types, ice and wrecks. Each finding writes a log entry, and about three entries open a new site or intel on the war map, following the Outer Wilds pattern.
  4. Triangle-rule layouts. Use the real MOLA relief to put a mesa or crater rim between the start and every point of interest. Place probes and caches on high points, and keep only one or two pulls in view at a time.
  5. Slope and heat cost. Climbing slows a HAWC, adds heat and drains cells faster, so the player has to choose between going over the ridge and walking around it.
  6. The Arsia skylight. In a pilot-only mission, park the HAWC at the rim and jetpack down an 80 m wall. Find what Darken hid in the tube and climb back out before they find your empty hull. This uses G-NOME's on-foot objectives and the core fantasy at the same time.
